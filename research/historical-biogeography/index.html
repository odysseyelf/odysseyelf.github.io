Historical Biogeography and Macroevolution

The geographic distribution of plant diversity reflects a long history of colonization, extinction, ecological adaptation, and lineage diversification. My research investigates how these processes interact with geological and climatic change to shape the assembly of regional floras and global biodiversity patterns.

I integrate phylogenomics, divergence-time estimation, ancestral-range reconstruction, trait-dependent biogeographic models, paleoenvironmental data, and macroecological analyses. A central goal is to understand not only how lineages disperse across geographic barriers, but also why some colonists persist and diversify whereas others leave little evolutionary legacy.

Ongoing Research

How did ecological opportunities shape the global assembly of Berberis?

This project reconstructs the macroevolutionary history and global assembly of Berberis, a widespread montane plant lineage distributed across the world’s major mountain systems. By integrating phylogenomics, ancestral-range reconstruction, and paleo-niche modeling, we examine how dispersal, ecological strategies, and environmental change generated its present-day distribution.

Our results indicate that Berberis originated in the Northern Hemisphere during the late Eocene, approximately 34.5 million years ago. Evergreen leaves and blackish-violet fruits were reconstructed as the ancestral states, whereas deciduousness and red fruits evolved later within the lineage.

The global distribution of the genus appears to have been shaped more strongly by temporally structured ecological opportunities than by individual dispersal-related traits such as fruit color. Long-distance dispersal was highly context dependent, with ecological windows created by global cooling, mountain building, and the expansion of temperate habitats facilitating the establishment and persistence of lineages after arrival.

Regional assembly also followed contrasting pathways. Present-day diversity in Asia and South America primarily arose through extensive in situ diversification following rare early colonization events. Europe and the western Pacific islands, however, accumulated lineages through more frequent immigration associated with the expansion and connectivity of temperate montane environments.

These findings suggest that the evolutionary signature of dispersal depends less on movement alone than on whether suitable habitats remain available for colonizing lineages to survive, establish, and diversify.

Global Ecology and Biogeography

How do polyploidization and long-distance dispersal jointly shape global plant diversification?

Long-distance dispersal is central to the global distribution of biodiversity, while polyploidy may increase the ability of plant lineages to establish in new environments. Using the cosmopolitan yellowcress genus Rorippa, this study tested how these two processes interact across evolutionary time.

We reconstructed the biogeographic and speciation histories of 17 diploid and 41 polyploid species using plastid genomes and multiple nuclear loci. Trait-dependent biogeographic models showed that polyploids dispersed at substantially higher rates than diploids, with an estimated 5.6-fold increase under the best-supported model.

Long-distance dispersal generated the amphitropical distribution of Rorippa, and numerous diploid and polyploid species originated through transoceanic speciation events. Polyploidization following dispersal was more common than polyploidization preceding dispersal, showing that polyploidy can be both a facilitator and an evolutionary consequence of colonization.

The study reveals a synergistic relationship in which long-distance dispersal creates opportunities for genome duplication, while polyploidization can improve establishment and subsequent expansion. This framework helps explain how interacting intrinsic and geographic processes generate cosmopolitan plant distributions.

Was Indochina a macroevolutionary source for Asian paleotropical plant diversity?

The Asian paleotropics contain exceptional species richness and endemism, but the evolutionary processes responsible for this diversity remain incompletely understood. This study used a time-calibrated whole-plastome phylogeny of Typhonium sensu stricto to test whether Indochina acted primarily as a source or a sink of tropical plant lineages.

Typhonium sensu stricto was inferred to have originated in Indochina during the early to middle Miocene, approximately 17.2 million years ago. Most diversification within the genus began soon afterward, with accelerated lineage accumulation at around 15–17 million years ago.

This increase in diversification coincided with the intensification of the Asian monsoon. The underground tuberous stem characteristic of the lineage may have facilitated adaptation to increasingly seasonal environments, while peripatric speciation contributed to the diversification of widespread species complexes.

The results identify Indochina as an important center of lineage origin and in situ diversification rather than merely a recipient of surrounding floras. They also illustrate how climatic seasonality, functional traits, and geographic isolation jointly contributed to the assembly of Asian paleotropical biodiversity.

What shapes the global distribution of spiny woody plants?

Spines are a major ecological innovation in plant defense, but they have evolved repeatedly from different organs and may provide different advantages across environmental gradients. This study examined the spatial and phylogenetic distribution of spiny woody plants across China, South Africa, and Australia.

Spiny species accounted for approximately 12% of woody plant richness, although their occurrence was strongly concentrated in particular evolutionary lineages. The proportional richness of spiny plants increased in drier environments and on soils with higher clay content, but declined where soil nitrogen availability was greater.

Different spine types showed distinct associations with climate and soil conditions, suggesting trade-offs across productivity gradients, specialization within particular climatic spaces, and constraints on environmental adaptability.

Spiny plant richness was also positively associated with the estimated historical richness of large herbivorous mammals. These patterns suggest that spines provide the greatest advantage where strong browsing pressure interacts with aridity or particular soil conditions, while different spine anatomies confer advantages under different ecological settings.

Future Directions

Future research will examine how dispersal, establishment, ecological persistence, and diversification interact across spatial and temporal scales. I am particularly interested in identifying the environmental conditions that create temporary ecological windows for range expansion and determining why these opportunities produce major radiations in some lineages but not in others.

This research program will integrate phylogenomics, fossil and paleoenvironmental evidence, trait-dependent biogeographic models, ecological niche reconstruction, and macroecological data. The broader goal is to develop a process-based understanding of how Earth history and lineage-specific ecological strategies jointly shape regional floras and global biodiversity.
