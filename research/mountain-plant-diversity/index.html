Origins and Assembly of Mountain Plant Diversity

Mountains support exceptionally high levels of plant diversity, yet similar patterns of species richness can arise through very different evolutionary and biogeographic processes. My research examines how geological history, colonization, ecological opportunity, and lineage diversification interact to shape the assembly of mountain floras.

By integrating phylogenomics, divergence-time estimation, historical biogeography, diversification analyses, functional traits, and ecological data, I compare plant radiations across mountain systems. A central goal is to understand why some regions generate diversity primarily through rapid in situ diversification, whereas others accumulate species through repeated immigration and long-term persistence.

Ongoing Research

What drives the remarkable diversity of leaf forms in Andean Berberis?

Species of Berberis in the Andes exhibit extraordinary variation in leaf morphology. What ecological functions do these diverse leaf forms serve, and how have they evolved throughout the evolutionary history of the genus?

This project integrates geometric morphometrics, phylogenetic comparative analyses, and ecological data to investigate the adaptive significance of leaf-shape evolution and its relationship to the exceptional species diversity of Andean Berberis.

Nature Communications

Why is plant diversity unevenly distributed across mountain regions?

By comparing Berberis radiations in the Andes and the Hengduan–Himalaya Mountains, this study asks why two mountain systems with substantial uplift histories harbor strikingly different levels of plant diversity.

Asian lineages evolved a key synnovation involving the sequential evolution of deciduousness and small, densely veined leaves. This trait combination expanded their fundamental niche during the Miocene and enabled colonization of cold, high-elevation habitats as alpine environments expanded in the Hengduan–Himalaya Mountains.

In contrast, Andean barberries remained predominantly evergreen and were largely restricted to warmer climatic niches. These findings show that exceptional biodiversity emerges through the interaction between intrinsic functional innovations and extrinsic ecological opportunities.

Future Directions

Future research will expand these comparisons across additional mountain plant radiations and geographic regions. I am particularly interested in testing how geological timing, ecological opportunity, functional innovation, and lineage history jointly determine whether mountain systems promote rapid diversification or accumulate diversity through repeated colonization.

This research program will increasingly integrate phylogenomics, comparative morphology, ecological niche evolution, and functional traits to identify general mechanisms underlying the origin and persistence of mountain plant diversity.
