Systematics and Phylogenomics

Reliable evolutionary inference begins with clearly defined species, robust phylogenetic relationships, and classifications that reflect evolutionary history. My research uses phylogenomic and integrative taxonomic approaches to resolve relationships among plant lineages, investigate conflicting evolutionary signals, and document previously unrecognized biodiversity.

I combine plastid genomes, nuclear target-capture data, genome skimming, morphology, divergence-time estimation, and methods for evaluating gene tree discordance. A central focus is understanding how rapid diversification, incomplete lineage sorting, and hybridization influence phylogenetic reconstruction and the classification of evolutionarily complex plant groups.

Featured Research

American Journal of Botany

How can incomplete lineage sorting obscure deep evolutionary relationships?

The systematic placement of Sladeniaceae sensu lato, comprising Sladenia and Ficalhoa, has long remained controversial. Plastid and nuclear data have supported conflicting relationships, while clear morphological synapomorphies uniting the two genera are lacking.

We combined genome-skimming and Angiosperms353 data to reconstruct plastid and nuclear phylogenies of Ericales, with comprehensive sampling of Sladeniaceae, Pentaphylacaceae, and related families. PhyTop and SNaQ analyses were used to evaluate incomplete lineage sorting and introgression, while divergence-time estimation provided a temporal framework for interpreting the observed phylogenetic discordance.

Plastid phylogenies supported the traditional inclusion of Ficalhoa within Sladeniaceae, whereas nuclear phylogenies placed Ficalhoa as sister to Pentaphylacaceae. The nodes involving Sladenia, Ficalhoa, and Pentaphylacaceae exhibited an exceptionally high level of incomplete lineage sorting, with an ILS index of 84.8%.

This conflict likely reflects the rapid early diversification of core Ericales during the mid-Cretaceous, approximately 110–105 million years ago. No evidence of introgression between Ficalhoa and Sladenia was detected, suggesting that stochastic sorting of ancestral polymorphisms was the primary cause of their conflicting phylogenetic placements.

The results support the placement of Ficalhoa in Pentaphylacaceae and demonstrate how rapid diversification and incomplete lineage sorting can obscure deep relationships along the backbone of major angiosperm clades.

Frontiers in Plant Science

How should Berberidaceae be classified in the genomic era?

Berberidaceae is a morphologically diverse early-diverging eudicot family with long-standing disagreements over generic boundaries. Depending on whether several major groups are treated broadly or divided into smaller genera, classifications have recognized between 13 and 19 genera.

To resolve these disputes, we assembled 23 new plastid genomes and combined them with available data from GenBank. After excluding problematic and redundant sequences, we reconstructed a phylogenomic framework based on 93 plastomes representing all 19 proposed genera of Berberidaceae.

The resulting phylogeny and divergence-time estimates supported the recognition of three subfamilies: Berberidoideae, Nandinoideae, and Podophylloideae. They also provided a rigorous evolutionary basis for recognizing nine tribes, including the newly proposed tribes Bongardieae and Jeffersonieae.

Our analyses supported a 19-genus classification, recognizing Alloberberis, Mahonia, and Moranothamnus as distinct from Berberis; Plagiorhegma as distinct from Jeffersonia; and Diphylleia, Dysosma, and Sinopodophyllum as distinct from Podophyllum.

Comparisons among plastid, nuclear ribosomal, and previously published phylogenies also revealed extensive conflict at different evolutionary depths. These patterns suggest that incomplete lineage sorting and hybridization have repeatedly influenced the evolutionary history of Berberidaceae, including possible ancient reticulation among the ancestors of Berberis, Mahonia, Alloberberis, and Moranothamnus.

Plant Diversity

How many plant species remain undiscovered in the Hengduan Mountains?

The Himalaya–Hengduan Mountains contain the greatest diversity of Berberis in the world, with more than 200 species recorded from China. Despite a long history of botanical exploration, many species remain poorly understood, incompletely mapped, or undescribed.

Through extensive fieldwork and examination of morphological variation, we identified seven previously unrecognized species from the northern Hengduan Mountains of northern Sichuan and southern Qinghai: Berberis chinduensis, B. degexianensis, B. jiajinshanensis, B. jinwu, B. litangensis, B. longquensis, and B. riparia.

The species were distinguished from one another and from morphologically similar regional taxa using combinations of vegetative and floral characters. Particular attention was given to floral structures, which provide important diagnostic evidence in a genus characterized by extensive vegetative convergence and morphological variation.

Plastome and partial nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences were used to evaluate the evolutionary distinctness of the proposed species and their relationships with previously described taxa. The study demonstrates the value of combining field-based taxonomy, comparative morphology, and molecular phylogenetics when documenting biodiversity in species-rich mountain regions.

These discoveries also show that even prominent and widespread montane plant groups remain taxonomically incomplete, emphasizing the continuing importance of species discovery as a foundation for evolutionary, ecological, and conservation research.

Future Directions

Future research will further integrate plastid genomes, nuclear target-capture data, population-level sampling, and morphology to resolve complex evolutionary histories across plant lineages. I am particularly interested in identifying when phylogenetic conflict reflects incomplete lineage sorting, introgression, ancient hybridization, or methodological limitations.

This work will also expand integrative taxonomic research in mountain biodiversity hotspots, where species boundaries often remain uncertain and undocumented diversity is still substantial. By connecting species discovery with phylogenomics and evolutionary process, I aim to develop classifications that are both taxonomically practical and consistent with the complex histories of plant lineages.
