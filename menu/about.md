**Mountains first drew me into biology.**

As a student, I spent much of my time exploring the mountains of Taiwan, where I became fascinated by the remarkable diversity of alpine and montane plants. That fascination eventually led me into plant systematics and evolutionary biology.

I received my Ph.D. from the Department of Forestry at National Taiwan University, where I developed a broad interest in plant diversity, evolution, and biogeography. Since then, my research has taken me from Taiwan to Southwest China and beyond. I first worked as a postdoctoral researcher in Xingyao Xu's laboratory at the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences, where I continued exploring the evolution and diversification of mountain floras across Asia. I am currently a postdoctoral researcher in Richard Ree's laboratory at the Field Museum in Chicago.

Today, I use phylogenomics, biogeography, and functional ecology to investigate how mountain floras originate, persist, and diversify through time. From Taiwan to the Himalaya–Hengduan Mountains and the Andes, my research explores the historical and ecological processes that generate plant diversity across the world's mountain systems.

Beyond academic research, I am also passionate about science communication and nature writing. During my years at National Taiwan University, I served as president of the NTU Mountaineering Club, an experience that further deepened my connection to mountains and field exploration. I enjoy sharing stories about plants, mountains, and natural history with broader audiences through essays and popular science writing, and I view scientific research and storytelling as complementary ways of understanding the natural world.
